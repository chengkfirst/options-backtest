## 1. Real EOD option chains, priced and checked against our own schema · data

**What:** Three vendors sell a one-time historical archive of US end-of-day option chains
with bid, ask and open interest. ORATS: "$599" once for 2007-to-present (roughly 500GB),
plus "$99/mo" to stay current. HistoricalData.net: "$799" one-time for February 2002 to
present, "$699" for a 10-year window, "$199" for a 1-year window, "$79/month" for ongoing
updates. DeltaNeutral (historicaloptiondata.com), which states it is an "OPRA-Licensed
Options Data Vendor": "$1,150" for 2002-to-present Level 1 (quote date, expiration,
underlying, strike, call/put, bid, ask, last, volume, open interest) and "$1,495" for
Level 2, which adds the greeks and implied volatility. Cboe DataShop sells the same from
January 2012, but prices through a calculator I could not read; the third-party figures
disagree — ORATS' own comparison page quotes "$2,100 once" for all symbols for one year and
"$8,500 once" for all symbols since 2012, while another summary quoted $1,500 a calendar
year and $7,200 for the archive.

**Link:** https://orats.com/near-eod-data · https://historicaldata.net/options.html ·
https://historicaloptiondata.com/

**What it means for this project:** `backtest/data/options/adapters.py` already declares the
target schema and `check_file()` already validates against it, so this is a purchase and a
`column_map`, not a build. But the vendors are not equally close to that schema, and the
distance is the entire cost of adoption:

- **HistoricalData.net maps by rename alone.** It ships "one file per trading day and one
  row per option contract", 34 columns, which is exactly the adapter's shape. `column_map`
  covers it: `quote_date` to `asof`, `underlying` to `symbol`, `expiration` to `expiry`,
  `type` to `right`, `underlying_close` to `underlying_price`; `bid`, `ask`, `volume`,
  `open_interest`, `iv`, `delta`, `gamma`, `vega`, `theta` already carry the adapter's own
  names. No code changes.
- **ORATS is cheaper and deeper but needs a reshape `column_map` cannot express.** Its row
  is one strike carrying *both* sides — "bid/ask prices (calls and puts)", call and put
  volume on one line — so there is no `right` column to map. It needs a melt before
  `FileChainSource` sees it: new code, not configuration. Its underlying price is also
  "derived from put-call parity", not the traded close the schema asks for ("the traded
  price, not the adjusted one"), so `underlying_price` would be a forward-implied spot and
  the moneyness computed from it is not the one a person reading the config would assume.
- **Do this:** buy HistoricalData.net's "$199" 1-year window and run `check_file()` on it.
  That is a $199 test of the whole adapter path — column names, right aliases, IV units (the
  schema wants a decimal, and the vendor ships `iv`, `iv_bid` and `iv_ask`), theta's sign
  and its per-calendar-day convention — before committing $799. If `check_file()` passes on
  one year, the archive is a rename away, and every option-side research question currently
  recorded as unanswerable becomes answerable.

One caveat on the cheapest good fit: HistoricalData.net is anonymous. Its methodology page
claims a reconciliation against records it did not produce — "127 of 127" sample SPX
underlying closes matching Cboe's published index levels, and 499,602 of 502,795 checks
agreeing (99.4%) in its latest session. That is the right *kind* of claim for a project
organised against silent failure, but it is the vendor's claim about itself, and it names no
operator and no OPRA licence. DeltaNeutral states an OPRA licence at the same archive depth
for $696 more, and that difference is what the money buys.

**How confident:** read it myself — each vendor's own page for the prices and field lists,
read against `SCHEMA` in `adapters.py`. The Cboe price is the exception and is reported as a
disputed range precisely because I could not read the calculator.

## 2. Autonomous Quant Researcher — an open-source loop built to be hard to fool · projects

**What:** A paper (4 Nov 2026, Henry Zhang) describing an open-source agentic research loop
whose stated premise is "the model proposes, and only trusted code decides." Its claims:
proposals are "strict declarative JSON over a fixed vocabulary, so there is no model-written
code to sandbox"; evaluation runs on immutable chronological stores split into discovery,
validation "and a final holdout that can be opened exactly once, after a durable receipt is
written"; acceptance is "a conjunction of absolute gates (positive edge and net P&L after
costs, a positive weekly-cluster bootstrap lower bound, survival at elevated costs, per-year
stability and sample floors), never a single score"; and "every formulation, refusal, crash
and result is kept in a hash-chained, append-only ledger." Campaigns covered "0DTE options
priced from traded option bars", overnight SPX verticals, daily ETF rules, earnings options
and others. Most hypotheses failed and are reported. The author calls the clearest success a
failure: "a language-model survivor that passed discovery and validation with Sharpe ratios
near 3 and fell to 0.26 on its single holdout use."

**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06607 · code at
https://github.com/henryzhangpku/autonomous-quant-researcher

**What it means for this project:** this is the closest published sibling to this project's
own stated reason for existing, and it names three mechanisms this project does not have:

- **A write-once holdout enforced by a durable receipt, not by discipline.** This project
  protects a hold-out by intention and convention. A receipt written before the holdout can
  be read, with a second read refused by code, is the same convention made into a guard —
  which is this project's own stated test of whether a convention is one.
- **A hash-chained, append-only ledger of every formulation, refusal and crash.** This makes
  the denominator of any multiple-testing correction a computed fact instead of a remembered
  one. The project has a config fingerprint; it has nothing that records how many
  configurations have ever been tried against a given hold-out, which is the number that
  decides whether a Sharpe means anything (see item 3).
- **"The model proposes, trusted code decides" as an explicit boundary.** The project already
  believes this — the platform never chooses a strategy, and `research-analyst` writes a spec
  rather than building. Declarative JSON over a fixed vocabulary is a sharper version of the
  same line, and the right shape for the agents already in `.claude/agents/`.

It is also the only item this week that has tested options strategies on real traded option
bars, which makes it a genuine `/benchmark` candidate rather than a design to admire: its
option-data loading and its 0DTE pricing are the parts of this platform that are currently
synthetic. Caveat on all of the above: these are the author's claims about his own code,
reported as such, and the acceptance gates in particular are easy to state and hard to
verify from an abstract.

**How confident:** read it myself — full abstract verbatim, and I confirmed the code URL on
the arXiv page. I have not read the repository.

## 3. PROMOTE — anytime-valid promotion tests for an adaptive research process · papers

**What:** A paper (2 Nov 2026, Benhamou and five co-authors) whose premise is that research
agents "generate and monitor trading rules faster than a human can document the resulting
selection process" — candidates appear at data-dependent times, weak rules are abandoned,
promising ones are watched until they cross a threshold. PROMOTE "assigns every candidate
predictable testing wealth at birth and accumulates a cost-aware e-process only from
subsequent returns"; if lifetime allocations sum to at most one, a weighted Ville argument
bounds the probability of ever promoting a rule whose net score has always had nonpositive
conditional mean. Their reported audit: across 3,000 null paths on 24 panel-rule hypotheses,
"repeated unadjusted tests produce a family crossing in 94.5% of null paths, versus 0.0% for
PROMOTE"; when an agent was allowed to pick candidate identities and birth times from past
scores, the funded gate showed "1.77% false-family crossings across 3,000 programs". The
market replay "produces zero promotions after costs", and the authors state the certificate
"is not a certificate that an earlier edge remains profitable at deployment."

**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03384

**What it means for this project:** it says the obvious correction is the wrong one. A
deflated Sharpe ratio assumes a fixed number of trials, known before you look. This
project's actual research process is adaptive — `docs/research/plan.md` gains questions,
weak ideas get dropped, a promising configuration gets re-run next month with another year
of data. That is precisely the case a deflated Sharpe does not cover, and it fails in the
direction that flatters: the correction is computed from a trial count that the adaptive
search has already invalidated. So if the plan was "add a deflated Sharpe and be done", that
plan is wrong, and the 94.5%-versus-0.0% figure is the size of the error being made in the
meantime by looking repeatedly without any correction at all.

What is implementable here: an e-process accumulated only from returns *after* a candidate
is declared gives a verdict that is valid at every look, which turns "run it again later and
see" — the thing this project actually does — from a fallacy into a protocol. It needs each
candidate's birth declared and its wealth allocated at birth, which is a change to how
`research-lead` registers a question, not an engine change. The honest headline is also
worth carrying: zero rules survived costs in their market replay.

Two things to check before building on it: the abstract reports "median crossing session
2,327", and the data is public ETF and industry data, which together imply daily bars, but
the frequency is not stated and I am inferring it. No code release is mentioned.

**How confident:** read it myself — full abstract verbatim. The daily-frequency reading is my
inference from "session" counts, not a claim the paper makes.

## 4. Both vendors that document a snapshot time snap it before the close · data

**What:** ORATS states its chain is snapped "14 minutes before the close" (3:46 PM ET), and
gives the reason: "options prices tend to deteriorate in quality at the close." Cboe's
Option EOD Summary ships *two* snapshots per contract per day as separate columns — a
15:45 ET one and a true end-of-day one (`Bid 1545`, `Ask 1545`, `Bid EOD`, `Ask EOD`) — so a
buyer chooses which close they mean. HistoricalData.net added a `quote_time` column on
6 August 2026, "Last update time of the standing bid/ask quote, in ISO 8601 UTC"; it is
blank for everything before that date, which its own sample page confirms.

**Link:** https://orats.com/near-eod-data · https://datashop.cboe.com/option-eod-summary

**What it means for this project:** "T's close decides" names two different moments the
moment real data arrives, and the gap is not conservative. The chain carries its own
`underlying_price` ("spot at that moment"), separate from the equity bar's close. With a
15:46 chain, a regime signal computed on T's equity close is paired with greeks and a spot
from fourteen minutes earlier — so contract selection (2b) picks a delta that was true
*before* the information the entry decision used existed. On a session that moves sharply in
the last quarter hour, the run selects and prices against a chain that no longer existed at
the decision point. That is an optimistic bias concentrated in exactly the sessions that
matter, and nothing flags it: requirement 1.20 ("signal and fill never share a quote") is
satisfied, but the reverse defect — a quote strictly *older* than its signal — has no guard.
Worth a spec that decides which spot is authoritative, and a check that refuses a chain whose
snapshot time and the equity close disagree unless that has been declared.

Two consequences follow:

- **Cboe is the only vendor that lets this be tested rather than assumed.** Its two snapshots
  are the same contracts on the same day at two times, which turns "does the 15:45-versus-close
  choice change the answer" into a sensitivity test on a realism assumption instead of an
  argument. That is this project's own stated treatment of realism assumptions, and it is the
  argument for paying Cboe's higher price over ORATS'.
- **No vendor sells a real historical `available_at`.** `quote_time` exists only from
  6 August 2026 forward, and it is a quote-staleness timestamp, not a vendor delivery time.
  For the whole archive, `available_at` will be absent and the adapter's "assumed equal to
  `asof`, and the run records that it was assumed rather than supplied" path is what actually
  runs. That confirms the design — the fallback was the right thing to build — and it means
  the "assumed" flag will be set on every real backtest, so it had better be visible in the
  interface and not only in a log.

**How confident:** read it myself for ORATS' stated snapshot time, Cboe's field list, and
HistoricalData.net's `quote_time` note with its blank-sample confirmation. The bias argument
is my own reading of those facts against `adapters.py` and `docs/requirements.md` line 1.20,
not something any vendor says.

## 5. pandas 3.1.0 and 3.1.1 — several changes that alter a number, not raise · tools

**What:** pandas 3.1.0 (22 Oct 2026) and 3.1.1 (4 Nov 2026). Alongside new features
(`DataFrame.select()`, `pd.col()` expressions, a 2-3x faster and optionally parallel
`read_csv`) the release notes list backwards-incompatible changes, several of which change a
result rather than raise. Construction of a datetime or timedelta from numeric input now
defaults to microseconds rather than the input unit, so `pd.to_timedelta([1,2], unit="s")`
is now `timedelta64[us]`. `select_dtypes("datetime64[ns]")` now selects only ns resolution,
and dtype instances match exactly. `merge` and `Index.join` with `how="left"` or `"right"` on
differing dtypes now keep the source dtype instead of casting to a common one. Mixed
bool/numeric `sum`/`mean`/`min`/`max` with `axis=1` now go through NumPy and can overflow or
lose precision. `Timedelta.total_seconds()` now includes nanoseconds. `inplace=` is
deprecated across `drop`, `rename`, `reset_index`, `set_index`, `dropna`,
`drop_duplicates`, `sort_values`, `sort_index`, `eval` and `query`. NumPy 2.0.2 or later is
required.

**Link:** https://pandas.pydata.org/docs/whatsnew/v3.1.0.html

**What it means for this project:** this is the silent-failure class the project is built
against, arriving in its own core dependency. A join key quietly keeping a different dtype
is how two spellings of the same key stop matching with nothing raising — the exact failure
the English-only rule exists to prevent, now reachable through a `merge`. A column-selection
helper written as `select_dtypes("datetime64[ns]")` starts returning fewer columns and raises
nothing. A duration built from a number silently becomes microsecond-resolution and compares
wrongly against an ns bar index.

Concretely: pin pandas now, then upgrade behind a run of `python -m tools.check` with
`-W error::FutureWarning`. The guard most likely to catch the ones that change a number
rather than raise is the test holding the event-driven and vectorised engines row-identical —
but only if both engines are exercised on the same upgrade, so run it as a deliberate
upgrade pass rather than letting the version float. Expect the `inplace=` deprecation to
produce warnings across the codebase, which is noise, not a bug, and worth clearing in one
commit rather than drowning the signal from the rest.

**How confident:** read a summary — the whatsnew page was read by a search agent, not by me,
and I have not checked which of these the codebase actually hits. The list above is the
release notes' own wording for the changes; which ones bite here is unverified and is the
first thing to establish.

## Inputs for /explore

- **Buy the chain, or keep the synthetic fixture honest first?** $199 tests the adapter and
  $799 removes limit 1 for twenty-four years. The argument against is not the money: it is
  that every question currently *known* to be unanswerable becomes answerable-looking
  overnight, and this platform's statistics and overfitting discipline have never once been
  exercised against real skew and term structure. Which of the two goes first.
- **Which close is the close?** If the best real bid/ask is snapped at 15:46 and the equity
  close is 16:00, "T's close decides" names two moments. Pick one and guard it, or carry both
  and make a configuration declare which it means.
- **Is a deflated Sharpe the wrong tool here?** Item 3 argues the project's research process
  is adaptive, which is the case a fixed-trial-count correction does not cover. An
  anytime-valid e-process is the alternative, and it would make almost every result the
  project has produced look worse. That is the point, and it is the argument.
- **Should a hold-out be openable once, by code?** A durable receipt and a refused second
  read, versus the current protection by intention. The project's own rule is that a
  convention a tool cannot check is a wish.
